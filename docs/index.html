Treasury auctions coupons

How each note and bond auction went, the way sell-side auction recaps score it. Bid-to-cover is bids divided by the amount sold. Indirect bidders are mostly foreign central banks and funds bidding through dealers; direct bidders bid for themselves; primary dealers are obliged to bid and take whatever is left, so a high dealer share means weak demand. Each number is compared with the average of the previous 6 auctions of the same tenor: orange = weaker than usual (more for dealers, lower cover), which tends to push yields up; teal = stronger. The real tail (high yield versus the 1pm when-issued yield) needs WI quotes that aren't free, so it isn't shown. Upcoming rows appear once Treasury announces the auction, usually a week before.

How Fed pricing is computed
  • ZQ (30-day fed funds futures) price = 100 − that month's average EFFR.
  • Meeting months: monthly average = (days before × old rate + days after × new rate) ÷ days in month, solved for the post-meeting rate.
  • If fewer than 7 days remain after the meeting, the next month's contract is used instead, since that whole month is at the post-meeting rate.
  • Meetings are chained: each post-meeting rate is the next meeting's starting point, starting from today's EFFR.
  • Each meeting's implied move ÷ 25bp is split between the two nearest outcomes, then chained from today's target range to get the probability of each range after each meeting (the same method as CME FedWatch).
  • Futures only pin down the average, which is why the site leads with bp priced. The odds additionally assume each meeting's move is independent of the previous one; in reality a hike in one meeting makes the next less likely, so the true distribution is probably more concentrated than shown. Option-based tools such as the Atlanta Fed Market Probability Tracker measure the shape directly. Prices also include a small risk premium.
  • ZQ prices come from Yahoo Finance (unofficial, delayed); compare with CME FedWatch.

Data: U.S. Treasury Daily Par Yield Curve · New York Fed EFFR · CBOT ZQ via Yahoo Finance. Updated automatically by GitHub Actions after each trading day's close.

Core PCE and Core CPI

The Fed targets 2% on PCE; core strips out food and energy. m/m is the raw monthly print (what the headlines report), shown for the last 2 years; 2% a year is about 0.17% a month. CPI for Oct 2025 was never published (government shutdown), so Oct and Nov 2025 have no CPI m/m bar. Annualized rates turn earlier than YoY. Dotted lines are headline (with food and energy), shown on the YoY and annualized views. Open diamonds are the Fed's own year-end projection (SEP median). Hollow bars (m/m) and dashed lines with open circles are the Cleveland Fed nowcast for months not yet published, from oil and gasoline prices and whatever CPI and PPI data are already out; hover for the value. Desks use it as the model's expectation going into a release. Trend compares core PCE with two measures that ignore the oddest items each month: the Dallas Fed's trimmed-mean PCE (drops the biggest movers at both ends) and the Cleveland Fed's median CPI (the middle item); when they sit below core, a few items are pushing core up. All YoY.

Breakdown

Services inflation without housing, the part most tied to wages. The PCE version (core services excluding housing and energy) is the one Powell means by supercore; the CPI line is CPI services less rent of shelter, a close approximation. Both YoY.

Inflation expectations

Market pricing from breakevens (Treasury yield minus TIPS yield), last 5 years. The 5y5y forward is the market's view of average inflation five to ten years out, past the current cycle; it is the Fed's main gauge of whether long-run expectations stay anchored, with roughly 2.2 to 2.5% counting as normal (breakevens run on CPI, which sits above the 2% PCE target). The 5y breakeven reacts to the current inflation story, such as oil and tariffs.

Deep dive

CPI vs PCE gap · PPI

What explains the CPI vs PCE gap

PPI

Producer prices. Several PPI parts feed straight into PCE, so desks use it to firm up the PCE forecast.

Unemployment and participation

Unemployment is the Fed's employment target. The right-hand line tells you the quality of a move. Participation: unemployment falling because people leave the labor force is weaker than it looks. Prime-age employment (share of 25 to 54 year olds with a job) is the measure many Fed officials and economists trust most, because it isn't dragged down by an ageing population or moved by who counts as looking for work. Open diamonds are the Fed's year-end projection (SEP median).

Nonfarm payrolls

Jobs added each month (thousands), last 2 years, with the 3-month average to smooth the noise. Each report also revises the two previous months, and the net revision often moves markets as much as the new month; hover a bar to see it. What counts as a good number has changed: with much lower immigration, the labor force barely grows, so payroll gains of roughly 0 to 50k a month are enough to keep unemployment steady (the breakeven pace, down from 150k or more in 2022-24). Judge a print against that, not against old norms.

Wages vs services inflation

Wages are not part of CPI, but they are most of the cost of services, so the Fed watches wage growth against supercore. Three ways to measure wages, all YoY: average hourly earnings (monthly, jobs report, distorted by who is working), the ECI (quarterly, the Fed's preferred, holds job mix fixed), and the Atlanta Fed tracker (median wage growth of the same people a year apart, 3-month average). Roughly 3 to 3.5% wage growth is consistent with 2% inflation.

Jobless claims

The most timely labor signal: weekly, out every Thursday. Initial claims are new layoffs; continuing claims are people still drawing benefits, so they rise when the unemployed take longer to find work. Low initial claims with high continuing claims is the "low hire, low fire" pattern: few layoffs, but hard to get hired.

Jobs surprises

Each release against what the market expected: the folded table lists the latest prints and what comes next; the chart is one indicator's history as bars (actual) and ticks (forecast). For unemployment and claims, a higher number is a dovish surprise.

JOLTS: openings per unemployed and quits

secondary

Openings divided by the number of unemployed (V/U) says how tight the labor market is: around 1 is balanced, near 2 in 2022 was overheated. The quits rate (share of workers quitting each month) tracks wage growth, because people quit when better jobs are easy to find. Noisy and about two months behind, so desks rarely trade it.

GDP and GDPNow

Real GDP growth, quarter on quarter at an annual rate (the headline number). The open bar is the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow model estimate for the quarter not yet published; it updates after each data release and is the number desks quote between GDP reports. Roughly 1.5 to 2% is the economy's trend pace now; sustained growth above that tends to push unemployment down and keeps the Fed wary. Open diamonds are the Fed's projection for growth over each year (Q4 over Q4), not a quarterly number.

Retail sales

Monthly change in retail sales, the first hard read on consumer spending (about two thirds of GDP). Desks focus on the control group: retail sales excluding autos, gas stations, building materials and restaurants, because that is the part that feeds straight into the GDP estimate. Headline swings with car sales and gasoline prices. Values are nominal, so part of each gain is just inflation.

Industrial production and factory surveys

Industrial production (Fed, monthly) measures factory, mining and utility output, YoY. The Philadelphia and New York Fed surveys ask manufacturers whether activity is rising or falling (above 0 = more say rising); they come out mid-month, before ISM, so desks use them to preview it. ISM itself is not free to redistribute, so it is only listed on the Calendar.

Calendar

FOMC meetings, minutes, the Chair's speeches and testimony, the data releases that move rates, and Treasury coupon auctions, month by month. Bold rows are the top-tier releases. FOMC rows show what futures price for that meeting; SEP marks meetings with new projections and the dot plot. Grey "blackout" rows mark when Fed officials stop talking about policy before each meeting (from the second Saturday before it until the day after). Auctions appear once Treasury announces them, about a week ahead. ISM dates follow ISM's rule (1st and 3rd business day) rather than an official list.
